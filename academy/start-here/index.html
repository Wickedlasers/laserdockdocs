How this course works

This course takes you from a new LaserCube to a safe first show. Most of it is about safety, because a Class 4 laser can blind someone in an instant.

  • Each lesson has the key points and a few questions, and most have a video or pictures. Get a question wrong? Pick another answer.

Class 4 laser

Lesson 1 of 10

Know your LaserCube

Before you switch anything on, learn what the parts on the back of your cube do. Most of them are there for safety.

Beginners guide, part 1: unboxingWatch on YouTube

The parts that keep the beam safe

  • Laser aperture: the window the laser light comes out of.
  • Beam block: blocks the beam and stops it from scanning into the audience. The screws on either side lock it.
  • Safety key: stops the laser from being switched on by accident. After a power loss, or when the interlock is opened and closed again, the key has to be reset.
  • E‑STOP (interlock) ports: two RJ45 sockets for the safety shut‑off. The safety jumper or an E‑Stop plugs in here. Never plug network (Ethernet) devices into them.
  • EMISSION LEDs: when they’re lit, the laser can emit. That doesn’t mean it is emitting right now, only that nothing stops it. Never cover them.
  • Safety eye bolt: when the cube is mounted up high, a safety cable goes through it.
  • Mounting bracket: for a secure installation. Screws lock the tilt.

The rest of the back panel

The power switch and power LED; the 19V DC power input and its charging LED (red while charging, green when full); the LCD and rotary knob for CubeOS; the network (LAN) port; the WiFi antenna connector; a USB port for a MIDI controller; the microSD card slot; audio in and out; DMX in and out; and the ILDA ports.

The label

The label on your cube shows its model and laser power, and the warning CLASS 4 LASER PRODUCT. Keep every factory label on the cube and readable: they are required for legal compliance.

Check yourself

Question 1What plugs into the two RJ45 ports marked E‑STOP?

These are the interlock ports. The manual says never to plug network devices into them; the LAN port is a separate socket.

Question 2The EMISSION LEDs are lit, but you see no beam. What does that mean?

Lit EMISSION LEDs mean the laser is ready to emit as soon as it gets a signal. Treat it as live.

Question 3You’re mounting the cube above a stage. What is the safety eye bolt for?

When the cube is mounted at height, a safety cable goes through the eye bolt. The tilt is locked with the screws on the bracket.

Lesson 2 of 10

Why laser light is different

A laser beam isn’t just bright light. This lesson explains why it can hurt someone when a much stronger stage light can’t.

The beam hardly spreads

Even a narrow stage light fans out by about half a degree. A laser beam can fan out by as little as 0.03 degrees, so it is still a tight spot full of energy long after it has left the cube.

Power on a tiny spot: irradiance

How much power lands on each bit of the beam’s area is called irradiance. Picture three lights that all use 20 watts. As an old filament bulb, 20 W makes a dim lamp. As an LED stage light, it makes a bright effect. As a laser, 20 W is an arena‑class projector. The power is identical; what changes is how tightly the light is squeezed together.

A beam that stands still is the most dangerous

Your LaserCube draws shapes by sweeping one beam very fast with tiny mirrors (scanners), and your eye blends the moving dot into a picture. Sweeping leaves the irradiance exactly as it was; what drops is the dose, because each spot only gets the beam for an instant. A single beam held still delivers everything to one spot, which makes it the riskiest output a laser has.

What it can do

  • Eyes: in the dark your pupil opens to about 7 mm, wide enough for a whole beam to get in. The eye then focuses it onto the retina. One accidental exposure can cause instant, permanent blindness.
  • Skin: a high‑power beam can cause burns.
  • Fire: lasers can set things alight a long way from the cube. Shine a 2 W torch at a candle and the wick doesn’t light; hold a 2 W laser beam on it and it catches.
  • Aircraft: a beam lights up a cockpit window and can distract a pilot or leave them briefly blinded, even many miles away.
  • Cameras and video projectors: a beam can badly damage them, the same way it can damage an eye for good.

Check yourself

Put these 20‑watt lights in order, from lowest to highest irradiance.

  1. Light bulb
  2. LED stage light
  3. Laser

All three use the same power; the laser squeezes it into the smallest spot, so its irradiance is far higher.

Question 1Same cube, three different outputs. Which carries the most risk?

A beam held still pours all of its energy into one spot, like sunlight through a magnifying glass.

Question 2Far from the cube, a laser beam is still hazardous when a stage light no longer is. Why?

The beam hardly widens, so its energy is still packed into a small spot a long way out.

Question 3Why can a beam hurt your eye so easily in a dark room?

In the dark your pupil opens to about 7 mm, wide enough for a whole beam, and the eye focuses it onto the retina.

Lesson 3 of 10

The safety rules

These are the rules that matter most, from the manual and X‑Laser’s operator course. Every later lesson builds on them.

Class 4 laser product

Avoid eye or skin exposure to direct or scattered radiation. Eye exposure can cause instant injury and permanent blindness.

Never at people

  • Never aim the laser at people, vehicles or shiny, reflective objects, and never let a beam be reflected toward people. Reflected (scattered) light can injure eyes too. The aperture label on the cube reads: do not aim the laser at people, vehicles or open airspace.
  • Never scan the audience. The LaserCube is not made for audience scanning, whatever the setup.
  • Don’t stand in front of the cube while it’s on. Wear laser safety glasses while rigging, maintaining or otherwise working with it.

Keep beams 3 metres above people

Wherever people may be, every beam must stay at least 3 metres (10 feet) above the floor, measured from the highest place someone could stand: a raised floor, a stage, a balcony.

Split every venue into safe areas, where people are and no laser light may reach, directly or reflected, and restricted areas, where nobody may be. Keep a buffer of at least 3 metres between them on all sides. Every beam has to end in a restricted area.

Fire, aircraft and who may use it

  • Lasers can start fires at great distances. Don’t use the cube near flammable materials.
  • Never let a beam hit an aircraft or go into the open sky. In the US, shining a laser at an aircraft is a federal crime punishable by fines or jail.
  • The LaserCube is not a toy. Keep it away from children and from anyone not trained to use it, before, during and after a show.
  • Never leave a running laser unattended, even with other crew around.
  • Don’t use the cube if you think it’s damaged or faulty, and never open or modify it. Email support@laseros.com instead.
  • The only maintenance allowed: cleaning the aperture window and the vents, with the cube switched off and the key out.

Check yourself

Question 1Where people may be, how high must every beam stay?

At least 3 metres, measured from the highest place people could stand, such as a stage or a balcony.

Question 2Your cube points away from the crowd, but a beam bounces off a mirror toward them. Is that OK?

A beam must never be aimed or reflected toward people. Every beam has to end in a restricted area.

Question 3Someone at a party asks you to sweep the beams through the crowd. What do you say?

Never scan the audience with a LaserCube, at any power. Keep every beam at least 3 m above people.

Lesson 4 of 10

Plan the projection area

Before you switch on, look at the room. Every beam has to end somewhere safe, and nothing it can reach should be a person, a reflection, a camera or anything that can burn.

Every beam must end on something

Laser beams travel a long way, so plan where each one stops: on a solid surface that doesn’t reflect and doesn’t burn.

Good places for beams to end

  • A concrete or brick wall
  • A projection screen
  • Heavy, opaque, flame‑retardant fabric
  • The cube’s own beam block

Never

  • Mirrors, windows and glass
  • Chandeliers, mirror balls, chrome, glossy panels
  • Trees and bushes
  • Roads, cars and other vehicles
  • The open sky

Look for hazards

  • Reflections: mirrors, glass, polished metal, mirror balls.
  • Things that burn: curtains and fabric that aren’t flame‑retardant, decorations, and any pyrotechnics. Keep them out of the beam area.
  • Lenses: cameras and video projectors. A beam can badly damage them, so keep beams away from them (lesson 8 shows how).
  • Height: find the highest place anyone can stand, such as a stage, a raised floor or a balcony, and keep beams 3 m above it.

Watch the whole area

For the whole show, someone who can stop the laser must have every beam, and every spot where a beam lands, in sight. If part of the room is out of your view, for example where beams land behind your position, put a second person there who can get the laser stopped at once. If anyone gets near the beams, say someone climbing onto a speaker, stop the laser straight away.

Outdoors

Beams must still end on something: a building, a screen, a wall. Never point them into the open sky or at vehicles: the cube’s own label says so.

Rain: the Ultra MK2 and Pro are rated IP51, or IP63 with the rain cover fitted. Light rain is OK only with the rain cover on; never run the cube in heavy rain.

Check yourself

Question 1You’re setting up in a hotel ballroom. Where can the beams end?

Beams must end on a solid surface that doesn’t reflect and doesn’t burn. Glass and chandeliers send beams off in other directions.

Question 2A video camera is recording from the back of the room. What do you do?

A beam can permanently damage a camera sensor, just like an eye. Keep it out of the beam area.

Question 3Mid‑show, a guest climbs onto a speaker and their head is near the beams. What do you do?

Stop first. Start again only when everyone is back where it’s safe.

Lesson 5 of 10

Mounting and the beam block

Where the cube sits decides where its beams can go. Mount it firmly, aim it, then set the beam block so no beam can reach people.

Beginners guide, part 2: first startWatch on YouTube

Mount it so it can’t move

  • Secure the cube before you use it, so the beam can’t shift by accident. Lock the tilt with the screws on the bracket.
  • On a truss or stand, pick a clamp whose rating covers the cube’s weight, and tighten it until neither gravity nor speaker vibration can tip the cube’s angle.
  • For any rigging overhead, run a safety cable through the safety eye bolt.
  • Handle it as precision equipment: some parts are fragile, so protect it from knocks and drops.
  • Leave open space on all sides so air can flow through the heat sink underneath, and run it between 10 °C (50 °F) and 40 °C (104 °F).

Set the beam block

The beam block is your hard stop: whatever the software does, the beam can’t get past it. Set it like this:

  1. Switch the cube off and take the key out.
  2. Aim the cube and lock it in place.
  3. Adjust the beam block, then lock it with the screws on either side.
  4. Switch on and project a full‑size test image at low brightness: the cube’s RGB‑THold test pattern draws a border round the projection area. Its size follows the Image Setup sliders, so check with the image at full size. Look where the beams go.
  5. Repeat until no beam can scan into any audience area, and every beam stays at least 3 m (10 ft) above people.

If the built‑in block can’t mask an angle you need, fix a piece of black foil (cinefoil) to the cube at the edge of the aperture, clear of the vents. Gaffer tape on its own is no beam block: a laser cuts right through it.

Put up warning signs

Place laser warning signs in and around the venue, especially where the audience can go.

Check yourself

Put the beam block steps in the right order.

  1. Switch the cube off and take the key out
  2. Aim the cube and lock it in place
  3. Adjust and lock the beam block
  4. Switch on and check where the beams go
  5. Repeat until no beam reaches the audience

Adjust with the power off and the key out, then check with the power on. Repeat until nothing scans into the audience.

Question 1The cube sits on a stand next to the speakers, and you notice its beams twitch with every kick drum. What do you do?

If the cube can move, its beams move too. Clamp it so gravity and vibration can’t change its angle.

Question 2The beam block can’t cover one corner you need blocked. What can you add?

Black foil stops the beam. Tape gets cut through, and clear plastic simply lets the beam pass.

Lesson 6 of 10

Key, interlock and E‑Stop

The laser only emits when the interlock is closed and the key is on. Know how to stop it instantly before you ever start it.

LaserCube and the emergency stop buttonWatch on YouTube

Outside the US: the safety jumper

  1. Plug the safety jumper into one of the two E‑STOP ports.
  2. Insert the safety key and turn it from OFF to ON. The EMISSION LEDs light up: the cube can now emit laser light.
  3. To stop emission, turn the key from ON to OFF and take it out.

In the US: the E‑Stop

US cubes come without the jumper. They must run with the E‑Stop that comes with them (an FDA requirement), and the laser won’t work without it.

  1. Connect the E‑Stop pendant to the cube.
  2. Turn the key on the cube to ON.
  3. If the red STOP button is pushed in, turn it clockwise until it pops up.
  4. Turn the key on the pendant clockwise to ON. The EMISSION LEDs flash slowly, once every 3 seconds.
  5. After the second flash, briefly press the green START button. The LEDs flash once a second; after 10 seconds they stay on and the laser can emit.

Always have two ways to stop

Set up every show so you have a main and a backup way to stop the laser instantly, and keep both within reach: for example the E‑Stop and the key, or without an E‑Stop the key and the power switch. Before the show, check that they really stop it.

Pulling the charger is not a way to stop: the Ultra MK2 and Pro keep running on their battery.

When the cube has lost power (switched off, or a flat battery), or after the interlock has been opened and closed again, the laser won’t start by itself: the key (and on US cubes the E‑Stop start sequence) has to be reset first. That’s on purpose, so a cube never fires the moment power comes back.

Check yourself

Put the US E‑Stop start‑up in the order the manual gives.

  1. Connect the E‑Stop pendant to the cube
  2. Turn the key on the cube to ON
  3. Raise the red STOP button (turn it clockwise)
  4. Turn the key on the pendant to ON
  5. After the second slow flash, press START briefly

After START the LEDs flash faster, then stay on after 10 seconds: the laser can now emit.

Question 1Outside the US and without an E‑Stop, what goes in an E‑STOP port?

The jumper closes the interlock. Without the jumper or an E‑Stop, the laser can’t emit.

Question 2The battery ran flat mid‑show. You plug in the charger and switch the cube back on, but the laser won’t emit. Why?

After losing power the cube waits for a reset, so it never fires the moment power returns.

Switch on and connect LaserOS

Now switch the cube on, find your way around CubeOS on its screen, and connect LaserOS.

Beginners guide, part 3: CubeOS overviewWatch on YouTube

Power and CubeOS

  • Charge through the 19V DC input. The charging LED is red while charging and green when full.
  • Turn the cube on with the power switch. The LCD shows CubeOS: turn and push the rotary knob to move through the menus.
  • The status bar at the bottom of the screen shows important messages and warnings.

Connect LaserOS over WiFi

  1. Make sure the supplied WiFi antenna is connected: the manual says to do this before using any wireless mode.
  2. On the cube, choose Control Mode → LaserOS.
  3. Choose Settings → Connection → WiFi server. Changing the connection mode restarts the cube: select YES and push the knob.
  4. On your phone or computer, join the cube’s WiFi. By default it’s called LaserCube and the password is Laser2020. You can change both under Settings → Network → WiFi Server.
  5. Open LaserOS. It finds the connected cube.
  6. With the interlock ready as in lesson 6 (jumper in and key on, or on US cubes the E‑Stop start done), turn the laser on from LaserOS’s power menu. On a computer, the ESC key turns it off again.

When WiFi isn’t good enough

  • Use the WiFi scan button in the WiFi server settings to see how busy the 2.4GHz channels are. If they’re congested, use a cable.
  • For several cubes, or where WiFi is busy, the manual recommends a network cable in LAN client mode. Without a router, the cube gives itself an address starting 169.254 (APIPA).
  • Get LaserOS for Windows or Mac from laseros.com. The phone apps stream to the cube; putting content on its SD card needs the computer version.

Beginners guide, part 4: connection modesWatch on YouTube

Check yourself

Put the steps for your first WiFi stream in the order the manual gives.

  1. Turn the cube on
  2. Choose Control Mode → LaserOS
  3. Choose Settings → Connection → WiFi server
  4. Join the LaserCube WiFi on your phone or computer
  5. Open LaserOS and turn the laser on

Connect your device to the cube first; LaserOS then finds it. The interlock must be ready, as in lesson 6.

Question 1You’re running four cubes in a busy venue. Which connection does the manual recommend?

For several cubes, or where WiFi is congested, use network cables in LAN client mode.

Question 2On a computer, which key quickly turns the laser off in LaserOS?

ESC turns the laser off. It’s a quick software stop; your E‑Stop and key are still your real stops.

Lesson 8 of 10

Safety zones

A safety zone limits where the cube’s beams go at full power. Use it on top of the beam block and the 3 m rule, never instead of them.

LaserCube and LaserOS safety zonesWatch on YouTube

Keep‑within and keep‑out

  • A keep‑within zone (the default) marks where beams may go. Its preview outline is green. Use it for the target area where beams can safely end.
  • Turn on invert and it becomes a keep‑out zone, with a red preview outline. Use it to keep beams off something, such as a camera or a video projector.
  • The zone can be a rectangle, circle or ellipse; sliders set its size and position.
  • Attenuation sets what happens to beams outside a keep‑within zone, or inside a keep‑out zone: 100% blanks them completely; 90% cuts them to 10% of their normal power.
  • The preview toggle projects the zone’s outline (the interlock has to be enabled).

Where you set it

In LaserOS control mode, LaserOS handles the safety zone, so the CubeOS entries are greyed out. In the cube’s own modes, such as Visualizer, set it on the cube under Settings → Projection → Safety Zone.

Check yourself

Question 1You want the show to stay inside the area above the stage. Which zone?

A keep‑within zone marks where beams may go; everything outside it is cut by the attenuation setting.

Question 2Attenuation is set to 90%. What happens to a beam outside a keep‑within zone?

90% attenuation leaves 10% of the power. Only 100% blanks the beam completely.

Question 3You stream from LaserOS. Where do you set the safety zone?

In LaserOS control mode the zone is handled by LaserOS; the CubeOS entries are greyed out.

Lesson 9 of 10

If something goes wrong

Know what to do before you need it: if a beam hits someone, if the cube is damaged, and which rules apply where you are.

If a beam hits someone

  1. Stop the laser with the E‑Stop or the key.
  2. Get medical help right away. For any beam in the eye, see a doctor straight away, even if it seems fine.
  3. Note it all down while it’s fresh: take photos of the room and of where the cube stood, then write down which cube it was, what was playing (colours and pattern), how far the person was from the cube, about how long the beam hit them, and the date and time.
  4. Email support@laseros.com with those details.

In the US: also tell X‑Laser (info@x-laser.com). An accidental exposure can be reported to the FDA on its form 3649.

If the cube is damaged

  • If you think it’s damaged or faulty, don’t use it, and never open it. Email support@laseros.com.
  • If it gives off smoke or any smell: switch it off and unplug it right away, keep it away from anything flammable, don’t charge or use it again, and email support@laseros.com.

The law where you are

It’s your job to know the rules where you use the cube. Laws vary: some places need extra licensing or registration, and many only allow laser shows under the supervision of a trained Laser Safety Officer (LSO).

In the US: an FDA variance is required to operate a LaserCube. A LaserCube bought in the USA comes with this variance, issued by X‑Laser. For details, such as a copy for a venue, ask X‑Laser.

If you see a laser show that looks unsafe, tell the venue, the company running it, and the authority that oversees laser shows where you are (in the US: the FDA and your state’s laser agency).

Check yourself

A beam has hit someone in the eye. Put the steps in order.

  1. Stop the laser
  2. Get them to a doctor right away
  3. Write down what happened and take photos
  4. Email support@laseros.com

Stop the laser and get medical help first; the details and the email come after.

Question 1Your cube gives off a strange smell. What do you do?

A cube should never smell. Don’t charge or use it again, never open it, and email support@laseros.com.

Question 2Who has to know the laser rules where you hold a show?

The manual makes it the user’s job. This course’s certificate is not a licence or permission to run shows.

Pre‑show checklist

LaserCube Academy · wiki.laseros.com/academy/start-here/

Would you run this show?

Six situations. Decide, then read why.

A house party. The cube sits on a table at chest height and sweeps beams across the room where people are dancing.

Beams must stay at least 3 m above people, and the LaserCube is never for audience scanning. Mount it higher and set the beam block first.

A club. The cube is clamped to a truss with a safety cable, and the beam block keeps every beam at least 3 m above the highest place anyone can stand, the DJ riser included. Beams end on a concrete wall, signs are up, you can see every beam, the E‑Stop is in your hand, a backup way to stop is tested and within reach, and you’ve checked the local rules.

Secured, cabled, beams 3 m above the highest place people stand and ending on a safe surface, signs up, every beam in view, a main and a backup way to stop within reach, and the rules checked. That’s a safe setup.

A garden party at night. For big beams, you point them straight up into the open sky.

Never let beams go into the open sky: they can dazzle or blind pilots miles away. Beams must end on something solid.

A wedding with a mirrored wall behind the dance floor. Your beams land on the mirror.

A mirror sends beams back toward people. Beams must end on something that doesn’t reflect.

A friend’s 12‑year‑old asks to run the show from LaserOS on their phone while you get a drink.

Keep the cube away from children and anyone untrained, and never leave a running laser unattended.

Your cube fell off a table yesterday. It still switches on, but something rattles inside.

Don’t use a cube you think is damaged, and never open it. Email support@laseros.com.
